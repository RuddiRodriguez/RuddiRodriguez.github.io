The banking problem: predict campaign response

A small historical disclaimer: I developed the original banking project before the ChatGPT era. No chatbot to ask why the SQL join doubled the rows—just me, the code, and some very patient debugging. This portfolio documents that experience: the problem I tackled, the decisions I made, and the skills I used. The reusable workflow shown here is a later evolution of that work.

I developed the original response-modelling project while working on a real problem in a bank: predicting which customers were more likely to respond to a campaign.

The operational question was who to prioritise within an eligible campaign population. A model score would support a ranked selection rather than treating every candidate as equally likely to respond.

This required connecting historical campaign contacts with observed outcomes and customer/account information, building a classifier, and making its predictions usable by the campaign process. The bank’s identity, internal systems, and business-specific names are omitted here.

Why imbalance made ranking the business objective

The campaign data was highly imbalanced: responders were a small minority. Predicting the answer for one particular customer was difficult, and a high overall accuracy could be misleading. A model that labelled everyone as a nonresponder could appear accurate while providing no useful campaign selection.

My practical objective was to rank the eligible population so responders were concentrated near the top. The business could then choose a percentage of customers to contact, balancing the responses captured against contact cost and campaign capacity.

Lift and gain were therefore central to evaluating the work. They made the selection trade-off visible instead of hiding it behind one overall accuracy figure. Gain asks how much of the responding population is captured at a given selection percentage; lift asks how much better that selection is than selecting randomly from the same population.

There was no single selection percentage that was automatically right for every campaign. Contact costs and the expected value of a response shaped the business choice. The model supplied the ranking and evaluation curves; the archived scoring code accepted a selection percentage and exported the selected identifiers. It did not calculate an economically optimal percentage.

Continue with evaluation through lift and gain and the cost-based selection decision. The methodology connects those decisions to the complete flow.

From historical records to a trained pipeline

The original development code builds a modelling table from contact history, accounts, and customer information. A separate response file supplies the observed outcome, matched to a customer and contact date.

Python derives earlier-response features, groups less common categories, creates calendar and campaign features, and prepares the modelling columns. The original scripts include train/test preparation, model experiments, validation, and a final fitting step.

The final-model script fits a LightGBM classifier inside a pipeline containing numeric scaling and categorical encoding, then serialises the fitted pipeline. That is the model-development path documented by the original code; the later reusable flow supports a broader set of algorithms.

Production setup: batch data preparation and scoring

The deployment folder contains two main responsibilities: preparing reusable data extracts and ranking the candidates for a campaign. It uses SQL queries, Python, local/shared-file storage, and a saved model pipeline.

  1. Extract: query contact history, active accounts, and customer information through an internal database-access helper.
  2. Prepare history: load the response file, match contacts to known outcomes, and exclude recent records while responses are still maturing.
  3. Cache: write history summaries, account data, and customer data to Parquet files.
  4. Score: read a campaign candidate CSV, join the cached features, apply the scoring preparation, and load the fitted pipeline.
  5. Select: sort positive-class probabilities from highest to lowest, take the requested percentage of candidate records, and export customer identifiers for the campaign process.

The code is a batch workflow, rather than a request-by-request prediction service. Its files show the preparation and scoring components; they do not establish the scheduling or monitoring arrangements used around them.

Make the model output usable for selection

The production ranking function removes the customer identifier from the model inputs, obtains the positive-class probability, sorts the scored records, and writes identifiers for the selected group.

This illustration preserves the scoring-and-ranking idea while replacing private identifiers and paths. It is not a replacement production script. The original implementation calculates the selection size from the input candidate population; rows removed during preparation may leave fewer scoreable records.

The export contains customer identifiers. The ranking is performed over candidate rows; the shown implementation does not explicitly deduplicate customers before selecting them.

Training and scoring need matching feature meaning

Deployment is also about the data contract. Feature names, category mappings, types, missing-value treatment, and time units need to mean the same thing in training and scoring.

The archived scripts make those responsibilities visible. Historical SQL measures contact intervals in days, while a production transformation computes recency in months. The production history summary also aggregates observed responses; it is not identical to taking the immediately preceding contact’s outcome. These details need review when reproducing or modernising the original model.

The example queries use contact dates for historical features and the scoring date for current-account features. That distinction is important, but joining current customer/account records does not alone guarantee a fully historical snapshot of every attribute.

How the real project inspired the reusable version

The original work brought together SQL feature construction, Python preparation, model fitting, evaluation, and batch scoring. The newer Tabular Classifier Flow makes much of that process reusable through shared utilities and project settings.

The banking use case explains why the portfolio focuses on ranked selections, cutoff comparisons, and reports—not just fitting a classifier. The SQL article shows how the original historical features were assembled; the other articles explain the reusable workflow that followed.

The saved charts in the validation article belong to the later project artifacts. They are not presented as original bank campaign results. No campaign uplift, production accuracy, or commercial impact figure is claimed here.

What this work demonstrates

This work connects database feature engineering, classification, saved model pipelines, and batch campaign selection. It demonstrates how I carried a prediction problem from historical data preparation into a practical scoring-and-export process.

Examples follow the project’s source logic. Bank-derived identifiers are anonymised where needed; excerpts are not standalone scripts. The full repository remains private.
