Classifying motion with machine learning

How I trained classifiers to distinguish diffusion from directed motion using simulated microscopy, then applied the selected model to real C. elegans tracks.

By Ruddi Rodriguez Garcia · Experimental imaging and machine learning

The machine-learning task

I built a supervised classification workflow to assign each tracked spot one of two motion labels: D, diffusion, or DM, directed motion. Experimental tracks lacked reliable labels, so synthetic images provided examples with known motion. Tracking those images produced the measurements used for training.

Each training row represents one trajectory, with seven measurements and its known class. I compared logistic regression and random forests, selected the model through cross-validation, tested it on held-out simulated tracks, and used the fitted model to classify real experimental trajectories.

The classifier receives track measurements extracted from microscopy. The images provide the experimental context and the synthetic training material; the model learns relationships between trajectory shape and motion class.

Why motion matters during the first division

The experimental movies in this project show Caenorhabditis elegans embryos during their first cell division. This small worm provides a way to study how a cell controls where it divides. Its first division produces two cells with different sizes and developmental roles.

The spindle—the structure that separates chromosomes—must be positioned correctly. Microtubules extend from its poles toward the cell's outer layer, called the cortex. Dynein, a molecular motor, helps generate pulling forces there. Differences in these forces contribute to the spindle moving toward the posterior side of the embryo. Research background.

In our collaborative 2018 study of cortical dynein, tracking revealed directed and diffusive-like motion. Diffusive-like cortical events were associated with force generation, and their frequency helped explain the force imbalance. Those conclusions relied on experiments and biological controls, beyond simply assigning a motion label.

This portfolio entry presents my separate machine-learning workflow for classifying motion. The paper documents the original imaging and simulation approach. Its published motion classification used trajectory asymmetry; the machine-learning comparison below is a later workflow, not the classifier used to produce the paper's results.

The difficulty: no known answers for the real tracks

A moving spot does not arrive with a reliable label telling us whether its motion is diffusion or directed motion. Short tracks, image background, and uncertainty in locating spots make visual judgement difficult. Without trusted experimental labels, we cannot train and measure a supervised classifier directly on the real recordings.

My approach was to generate synthetic microscopy images with known motion, designed to resemble the experimental conditions. The simulated motion supplies the labels: D for diffusion and DM for directed motion. We can then detect and track the simulated spots, calculate their measurements, and train a model using those labelled tracks.

This puts the image-to-track measurement step inside the procedure. The model learns from measurements obtained after tracking simulated images, rather than receiving perfect coordinates alone. Finally, the same kind of measurements from experimental tracks are passed to the trained model.

How we made realistic synthetic images

Supplemental Text 2.5 and Table S2 document the procedure. We simulated two-dimensional diffusion, with added drift for directed motion. Lifetimes followed an exponential distribution. Spot brightness resembled the experiment, Gaussian blur represented microscope optics, and Gaussian pixel noise plus fast-diffusing particles created background.

The documented examples contain 100 frames over 20 seconds, sampled every 0.2 seconds. Table S2 lists a Gaussian optical width of 0.3 µm, particle diffusion of 0.002 µm²/s, and drift components of 0.4 µm/s. Movies S8 and S9 use a signal-to-noise ratio of 5 and mean lifetime of 10 seconds.

The published procedure applied CANDLE denoising, Laplacian-of-Gaussian enhancement, and u-track linking before asymmetry-based classification. Figure S3 compared recovered positions, classes, speeds, and durations with simulation truth across noise levels. This tests the complete measurement procedure. The later Python repository instead compares supervised models using seven track measurements.

The paper also reports that short directed tracks can appear diffusive-like. Track duration therefore matters when interpreting experimental predictions. Published parameters describe the research simulation; they are not assumed to be verified settings for every archived TIFF or row in the machine-learning tables.

From images to motion labels

  1. Simulate images. Create known diffusive and directed motion under conditions intended to resemble the experiment.
  2. Detect and track spots. Export the trajectories. The archive includes TrackMate XML files.
  3. Measure each track. The original MATLAB stage calculates net displacement, efficiency, straightness, asymmetry, bending, skewness, and kurtosis. The recovered feature routine retains its original author attribution.
  4. Train and compare models. Python compares logistic regression and random forests. Missing-value handling and scaling are fitted inside cross-validation.
  5. Evaluate on held-out synthetic tracks. Their known labels allow a meaningful comparison between expected and predicted motion.
  6. Apply the fitted model to real tracks. Use the same seven measurements and fitted preprocessing. Save predictions separately from known labels.

The organised portfolio branch preserves this method: original notebook, MATLAB feature calculation, Python training functions, labelled synthetic tables, and real-data tables. The original simulation generator has not yet been recovered into this branch.

What each track tells the classifier

A list of spot positions becomes a small set of measurements describing how the spot moved. Together, they help distinguish a wandering path from persistent movement in a direction.

MeasurementInformation supplied to the model
Net displacementDistance between the first and last positions.
EfficiencySquared net displacement relative to the accumulated squared steps, accounting for the number of steps.
StraightnessHow consistently consecutive steps point in the same direction.
AsymmetryWhether positions spread along a preferred axis rather than equally in all directions.
BendingTurning behaviour along the trajectory.
Skewness and kurtosisShape of the position distribution along its main axis.

The MATLAB feature routine contains the exact formulas and retains its original author credit. Track identifiers are excluded from model inputs.

Training and choosing the model

The guided notebook uses 985 labelled simulated tracks: 389 D and 596 DM. The original random split reserves 197 tracks for the final test and uses 788 for training. Test tracks stay outside model selection.

Within the training set, five-fold cross-validation compares 267 combinations of model settings and missing-value strategies. Logistic regression learns a weighted relationship between measurements and class; random forests combine decision trees to capture more complex relationships.

Missing measurements are filled using mean, median, or most frequent values, and measurements are scaled with MinMaxScaler. Both operations sit inside the training pipeline, so each cross-validation fold learns its preparation from its own training portion.

The search records precision, recall, F1, and balanced accuracy. Selection uses macro recall: calculate the fraction correctly recovered within each class, then average the two classes equally. This gives diffusion and directed motion equal weight when choosing a model.

The selected model is a random forest with 1,000 trees, maximum depth 25, minimum 10 samples per leaf, maximum 10 leaves per tree, and median imputation. The executed notebook records the settings and results.

Inside the classification code: what happens during training

The following excerpts come directly from the organised repository. This is the training implementation, with each operation explained beside its code.

1. Separate measurements from known answers

The seven feature columns become the inputs. Classification contains the known D or DM label. Keeping them separate stops the answer from becoming an input to the model. The random 80/20 split is performed before fitting.

2. Build preparation and classification together

Pipeline applies three steps in order. The imputer fills missing measurements, the scaler puts columns on comparable ranges, and the classifier learns from those prepared measurements. Logistic regression is the starting classifier; the search below also replaces it with a random forest.

For each training fold, filling and scaling are learned only from that fold’s training rows. Its validation rows are transformed using those learned values. The final test set does not influence these choices.

3. Specify the classifiers and settings to compare

The first group tests logistic regression: two penalty types, four values of C, balanced class weights, and three ways to fill missing values. The second group tests random forests with different tree counts, depths, and leaf sizes. The model__ prefix identifies a setting belonging to the classifier step.

There are 24 logistic-regression combinations and 243 random-forest combinations: 267 in total. C controls the strength of the restriction on logistic-regression coefficients. Forest depth and leaf settings control how finely the trees divide the training examples.

4. Compare settings on training data

cv=5 divides the training set into five parts. For each setting, the pipeline fits on four parts and predicts the fifth, repeating until every part has served as validation data. This produces 1,335 candidate fits. The search averages their scores to compare settings.

recall_macro averages the recall of D and DM equally. refit selects the setting with the best average macro recall and fits that complete pipeline again using all 788 training tracks. best_estimator_ then contains the fitted missing-value handling, scaling, and chosen classifier.

5. Run training and inspect the selected model

This is the start of run_workflow(). search.fit(x_train, y_train) runs the entire comparison. Logistic regression learns coefficients relating measurements to motion labels; random forests learn decision-tree splits. Both use the known simulated labels during fitting.

The workflow records every candidate’s scores in model_comparison.csv. It reports best_params_ so the winning settings are visible, then evaluates the selected pipeline on the untouched holdout:

In the saved notebook run, selection chose a random forest with 1,000 trees and median imputation. The 197 held-out tracks are used to report final performance, not to select the winning settings.

6. Compare predictions with known labels

model.predict() applies the fitted preparation and classifier to the test measurements. The report compares its outputs with Classification. The confusion matrix counts correct labels and both kinds of mistake.

7. Apply that fitted classifier to experimental tracks

Real tracks go through the same fitted pipeline. Prediction does not retrain the model or recalculate its preparation from the experimental table. The original D_DM value is a placeholder; the output is explicitly saved as predicted_motion.

For logistic regression, the fitted coefficients determine the class decision from the prepared measurements. For the selected forest, each tree contributes class probabilities and their average determines the predicted label. No hand-written displacement threshold assigns D or DM in this machine-learning stage.

Follow the executed notebook for the complete sequence and saved outputs, or run_workflow.py for the script connecting these functions.

Classification results

The rerun of the organised notebook uses the recovered 985-track synthetic training table. Its original random 80/20 split leaves 197 tracks for testing. The selected model classified 190 of 197 correctly, or 96.45%. This result concerns held-out synthetic tracks only.

Two diffusive tracks were classified as directed and five directed tracks as diffusive. Macro recall was 96.60%. The confusion table makes both kinds of error visible instead of relying on accuracy alone.

The split is by track, not by source movie. It therefore does not establish performance on an unseen movie. Applying the fitted model to 193 usable real cortex tracks produced 88 D and 105 DM predictions. Those counts describe model output; without trusted experimental labels, they do not measure real-data accuracy.

The key assumption is that synthetic images capture enough of the experimental conditions for their learned motion patterns to transfer. Differences in background, spot visibility, localisation, and track duration can affect that transfer. Known synthetic labels help test the method; biological controls and labelled experimental examples are needed to establish what its real predictions mean.

This project shows how I connect an experimental question to image simulation, tracking, numerical measurements, and a reproducible learning workflow. The original research predates ChatGPT: the worms were dividing while we were still dividing our time between microscopes and MATLAB.
